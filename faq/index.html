q: what is a commitment?

suppose you want to make some kind of message predicting something on social media, but you don't want to reveal what that something is until it's already happened. you can commit your message, a process which generates two things: a commitment and a decommitment key. You publish the commitment to the world first, and after the thing you're predicting has happened you publish the decommitment key and the original message itself. Then, anyone with all three things can verify that the decommitment key and claimed message match your original commitment.

commitment schemes are hiding and binding. hiding means it's hard for people to learn meaningful information about the committed message just from the commitment (without the decommitment key). binding means it's hard for a guy to make two different pairs of message/decommitment key which verify to the same commitment.

q: why not just use SHA256(message || long random stuff) as the commitment scheme instead of this fancy stuff?

mostly down to the fact that i am more convinced by the security guarantees for pedersen than for that kind of hash-based stuff.

pedersen commitments are actually perfectly hiding. this means, no matter how much computing power an adversary has, it is theoretically guaranteed that having the commitment gives them zero information about the committed message.

on the other hand, the hiding security of SHA256(message || long random stuff) is less clear. it's at best computationally binding (i.e. the commitment technically does leak some information about the message, but it's computationally difficult for anyone to meaningfully leak a single bit of it). however, for the proof to even work, you have to model SHA256 as a random oracle. addendum: both pedersen commitments and SHA256(message || long random stuff) are computationally binding, modulo some assumptions. this version of a pedersen commitment is computationally binding, as long as elliptic curve discrete log is hard and SHA256 is collision resistant. the hash thing is computationally binding as long as SHA256 is collision resistant. collision resistance and elliptic curve discrete log are standard assumptions that i am more confident in, compared to assuming SHA256 "behaves like" a random oracle.

q: how is H chosen exactly?

it's a NUMS (nothing up my sleeve) value that I took from other pedersen commitment implementations, like this one. you encode the generator point of secp256k1 in compressed SEC1 form, hash it with SHA256, and take the 256 bit digest as the x-coordinate of H (and solve for the y-coordinate from that).

it's important to disclose how H was chosen exactly, as the computational binding security of this implementation of pedersen commitments hinges on how hard it is to compute an elliptic curve discrete log of point H with base point G. otherwise i could just pick H = kG for some k only i know, which would allow me to forge any message/decommitment key pair i want for any commitment. incidentally this is similar to how the Dual_EC_DRBG backdoor works.
